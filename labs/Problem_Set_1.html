Did Neighborhood Bonding Social Capital Differ Between Central and Outer Shanghai?

The question：

Did Neighborhood Bonding Social Capital Differ Between Central and Outer Shanghai?

The data

This analysis uses data from the 2022 Shanghai Lockdown Study, an online survey conducted between April 29 and June 1, 2022. The survey was distributed in Chinese through Wenjuan Xing to adults who were in Shanghai during the lockdown. Participants were recruited through purposive sampling, with a target of approximately 200 respondents in each of Shanghai's 16 districts.

The analysis uses 2,795 respondents with available geographic coordinates. These coordinates allow each respondent to be assigned to a district through a spatial join. The findings therefore describe the respondents included in this analytical sample, rather than representing all residents of Shanghai.

LAB04_shanghai-districts.geojson contains polygons representing Shanghai's sixteen administrative districts. Its attributes include official administrative codes, Chinese and English district names, and a survey_code field used to compare survey records with spatial assignments.

The main outcome variable is Social capital-bond. Its values range from 4 to 20, with higher values representing stronger neighborhood bonding social capital. The geographic unit is the administrative district. The 16 districts are divided into two groups:

Central Shanghai (7 districts): Huangpu, Xuhui, Changning, Jing'an, Putuo, Hongkou, and Yangpu.

Outer Shanghai (9 districts): Pudong, Minhang, Baoshan, Jiading, Songjiang, Qingpu, Fengxian, Jinshan, and Chongming.

The comparison uses the mean bonding social capital score among respondents in each region. Each respondent contributes equally to the regional mean.

The method

The analysis has four steps.

1. Assign. Each respondent’s GPS point was matched to the district polygon that contains it.

2. Summarize. For each of the 16 districts, I obtained the number of respondents, the total score and the mean score (Summarize Within in ArcGIS Online).

3. Group. Central Shanghai consists of seven districts: Huangpu, Xuhui, Changning, Jing’an, Putuo, Hongkou and Yangpu. Outer Shanghai consists of the other nine: Pudong, Minhang, Baoshan, Jiading, Songjiang, Jinshan, Qingpu, Fengxian and Chongming.

4. Compare. A region’s mean is its total score divided by its number of respondents, so every respondent counts equally and small districts, such as Chongming (n = 67), do not outweigh large ones, such as Pudong (n = 280). I tested the difference between the two regional means with Welch’s t-test, which does not assume equal variances, and reported the effect size as Cohen’s d.

What I found

Central districts scored higher than outer districts, the opposite of what I expected. The mean score was 11.57 in central Shanghai and 11.18 in outer Shanghai, a difference of 0.39 points (Table 1). This difference is statistically significant (Welch t(2,328) = 3.46, p = 0.0006) but small: it is about 3.5% of the outer mean, and Cohen’s d is 0.13. Both regions score slightly below the scale midpoint of 12.

The district-level results show that the two regions overlap a great deal. Outer districts range from 10.26 to 11.90 and central districts from 11.07 to 11.81, so the variation inside each group is larger than the 0.39-point gap between them. The three lowest districts are all outer districts: Fengxian (10.26), Qingpu (10.65) and Jiading (10.89). The highest mean belongs to an outer district, Chongming (11.90), but it rests on only 67 respondents and has the widest confidence interval (±0.85).

The analysis shows a small difference in average neighborhood bonding social capital between central and outer Shanghai.

The mean score for central Shanghai was 11.57, based on 1,081 respondents across seven districts. The mean score for outer Shanghai was 11.18, based on 1,714 respondents across nine districts. The difference between the two regional means was 0.39 points, with central Shanghai scoring higher.

This result is opposite to the expected direction proposed in the presentation. However, the difference is relatively small, representing approximately 3.5% of the outer Shanghai mean.

There was also considerable variation among individual districts. In outer Shanghai, district-level means ranged from 10.26 to 11.90. In central Shanghai, they ranged from 11.07 to 11.81. Fengxian had the lowest district mean at 10.26, followed by Qingpu at 10.65 and Jiading at 10.89. Chongming had the highest mean at 11.90, although its result was based on 67 respondents.

These results suggest that variation between individual districts may be more substantial than the difference between the two broader regional groups.

Sensitivity Analysis

I changed two analytic decisions, one at a time, and compared each result with my original analysis (GPS-matched districts, mean score; central 11.57, outer 11.18, difference +0.39, p = 0.0006).

First, I replaced GPS-matched districts with the districts respondents reported. The two rules placed 95.6% of respondents in the same region, and the difference was +0.47 (central 11.60, outer 11.13; p < 0.001), slightly larger than the original.

Second, I compared the share of low scorers instead of means. Below the sample median (12), 45.8% of central and 51.3% of outer respondents were low (−5.6 percentage points); in the bottom quartile (≤ 9), the shares were 23.9% and 29.9% (−6.0 points). Both gaps were significant (p = 0.004 and p < 0.001).

What this does not show

The gap is statistically significant, but it is small (d = 0.13). With 2,795 respondents, even a small difference can reach significance, so I would not read it as a large one. The two regions also overlap a lot, and the variation inside each group is bigger than the gap between them, which means a regional average says little about any single resident.

There are also limits in the data. I left out 553 respondents (16.5%) because their coordinates were missing, although their average score was similar to everyone else’s. I do not know how respondents were recruited, so the results describe these respondents and not necessarily all of Shanghai. Some districts are small as well; Chongming has only 67 respondents.

Finally, splitting 16 districts into central and outer was my own decision, and a different cut-off could change the result. The sensitivity analysis in Section 5 tests how respondents were assigned to regions and how the outcome was defined, but not this cut-off. And because I did not control for other characteristics, I cannot say that living in central Shanghai causes higher bonding social capital.

These limitations do not make spatial analysis useless. They define the claims the analysis can reasonably support and the uncertainty that should accompany those claims.
